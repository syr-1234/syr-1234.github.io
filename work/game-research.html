Freedom by design

A comparative study of how two games construct the player’s identity. The Last of Us takes the final choice out of the player’s hands; Cyberpunk 2077 offers freedom that turns out to be shaped by the systems of Night City.

Theory
Foucault: sovereign power, biopower

The argument

In the final scene of The Last of Us, players must make Joel kill the Firefly surgeon and take Ellie away, whether they agree or not: a sovereign power drawing ethical boundaries inside a linear narrative.

Cyberpunk 2077 gives players control over appearance, skills, combat style and key branching choices. Yet that freedom is bounded by Night City’s logic of capital, violence and technology. V cannot escape the Relic chip, and Johnny Silverhand keeps intruding on V’s memories, emotions and body. The player’s “self” is continually rewritten, closer to Foucault’s biopower: not direct command, but the gradual shaping of a subject through systems, technology and environment.

What it changed

Game narrative is not only storytelling. It builds the player’s experience and understanding through rules, interaction, perspective, feedback and immersion. This is the direction I want to take into practice: interactive narratives where players’ choices genuinely help generate meaning.
