This page collects evidence from surveys, platform observation, and public reports to show how women's health content is restricted, hidden, or treated differently across digital platforms.

1. Survey Results

We collected 59 responses via an online questionnaire (Google Forms and WJX). Most participants were between 18 and 25 years old.

  • More than 90% of participants saw women's health content removed or hidden at least occasionally.
  • 40.7% of participants saw this happening frequently.
  • Participants identified vaginal health (78%) and sex education (76%) as the two most restricted topics.
  • 37.3% of people posting content have limited reach or a sudden drop in engagement.
  • 69.5% of participants thought this censorship was unfair.
  • 93.2% of participants believed this affects public access to women's health information.
  • 76.3% of participants are willing to participate in advocacy activities, mainly by sharing posts.

Source: Our online survey, n=59 (2026)

2. Platform Observation

APP: Xiaohongshu

Case 1 - Low Views

One user posted “Why people get cancer in Wyoming.” The full name of the disease had been replaced with an abbreviation. The post received very low engagement, with only a few likes.

Case 2 - Repeat Limitation

A female anchor shared a video titled: “Restricted 3 Times—Why I Keep Sharing My PCOS Pregnancy Journey.” Despite multiple restrictions, she continued to post.

Case 3 - Words Are Abbreviated

Topics related to women's health on the platform often need to be replaced by abbreviations before they can pass review and be published.

Case 4 - Double Standards

Posts on the same platform about men's health topics use full, uncensored language. No similar limitations were observed.

Case 5 - Content Comparison

The number of likes for men's health content on the same platform is much higher than the number of likes for women's health content.

3. Reports and News Articles

Case 1 - Instagram's “Period Wars” (The Daily Beast, 2015)

One user posted a photo holding a bloody menstrual cup to protest an account ban for period-related content. Instagram later confirmed that menstrual bleeding was not banned. However, the user received violent messages and death threats. The incident shows that period stigma remains high online even when platform rules do not explicitly prohibit it.

View article

Case 2 - Women's health is a “dirty secret” (HANX, 2025)

Women's health brand HANX found that up to 80% of ads for condoms, which are approved medical devices, were restricted or banned on the Meta platform. However, advertising of erectile dysfunction drugs is expressly approved. The company stated that women's health is not taboo, not inappropriate, and is vital health care.

View article

Case 3 - Meta London Office Protest (Banbury Guardian, 2023)

Bodyform organized a protest after ads using words like “vagina” and “menstrual cycle” were rejected as sexual content. Demonstrators held signs that read “Get Ovary it Meta.” Bodyform's research found that around 40 words related to women's health, including menopause, miscarriage, and vulva, are censored or banned on social media.

View article

4. What Do These Cases Illustrate?

Across platforms and countries, women's health information is often treated as sensitive, inappropriate, or even obscene, while similar men's health topics are not restricted in the same way. This is not random. This is a pattern.
